## RLHF: Teach It With Thumbs Up, Not Rules

<Convo
  lines={[
    { who: 'cherry', text: 'How do I reward behaviour that I cannot write a formula for?' },
    { who: 'sriku', text: 'Ask people. Google defines reinforcement learning from human feedback as using feedback from human raters to improve the quality of a model’s responses. For example, users rate a response with a thumbs up or thumbs down, and the system adjusts its future responses.' },
    { who: 'sundu', text: 'And the research version?' },
    { who: 'sriku', text: 'Christiano, Leike, Brown, Martic, Legg and Amodei, in 2017. They define goals in terms of non-expert human preferences between pairs of trajectory segments, rather than a hand-written reward function.' },
  ]}
/>

<Gif
  name="rlhf"
  alt="Four boxes show the loop: a model writes answers, humans rank them, a reward model learns the ranking, and the policy is updated to earn reward. Three answers are ranked from best to worst. A caption quotes the paper that human feedback on under one percent of the agent's interactions was enough for Atari and robot tasks. The loop is a toy illustration."
  caption="Fig. 121: The RLHF loop in four boxes."
/>

<Convo
  lines={[
    { who: 'cherry', text: 'Did it work?' },
    { who: 'sriku', text: 'They show it can effectively solve complex RL tasks without access to the reward function, including Atari games and simulated robot locomotion, while providing feedback on less than one percent of the agent’s interactions with the environment.' },
    { who: 'sundu', text: 'What is that in human time?' },
    { who: 'sriku', text: 'The authors say it reduces the cost of human oversight far enough to be practical, and that they trained complex novel behaviours with about an hour of human time.' },
    { who: 'cherry', text: 'And for language models?' },
    { who: 'sriku', text: 'The same recipe came back for text. Part F’s InstructGPT used labeler rankings. In 2020 Stiennon and colleagues trained a model on human comparisons between summaries, and used it as a reward function to fine-tune a summarization policy with reinforcement learning.' },
  ]}
/>

<CaseStudy
  title="Deep reinforcement learning from human preferences (Christiano et al., 2017)"
  stats={[
    { value: 'Under 1%', label: 'Of the agent’s interactions needed human feedback' },
    { value: '~1 hour', label: 'Of human time for complex novel behaviours' },
  ]}
  winner="The abstract: “we explore goals defined in terms of (non-expert) human preferences between pairs of trajectory segments.”"
  takeaway="It is often easier to say which of two outputs is better than to write the rule that explains why."
  source={{
    text: 'Christiano, Leike, Brown, Martic, Legg and Amodei, “Deep reinforcement learning from human preferences”, 2017. Free on arXiv.',

**Real definition:** using feedback from human raters to improve the quality of a model’s responses. For example, an RLHF mechanism can ask users to rate the quality of a model’s response with a 👍 or 👎 emoji, and the system can then adjust its future responses based on that feedback. [Google’s glossary: Reinforcement Learning from Human Feedback (RLHF) ↗](https://developers.google.com/machine-learning/glossary#reinforcement-learning-from-human-feedback-rlhf)

## ROUGE: A Ruler for Summaries

<Convo
  lines={[
    { who: 'cherry', text: 'How do I score a thousand summaries without reading them all?' },
    { who: 'sriku', text: 'With ROUGE, a family of metrics for automatic summarization and machine translation. ROUGE determines how much a reference text overlaps with a model’s generated text, and higher scores mean more similarity. Each member measures overlap differently, and typically reports precision, recall and F1.' },
    { who: 'sundu', text: 'Why is recall in the name?' },
    { who: 'sriku', text: 'It stands for Recall-Oriented Understudy for Gisting Evaluation. The glossary says BLEU optimizes for precision and ROUGE for recall, which is why BLEU suits translation and ROUGE suits summarization. BLEU was in Part B.' },
  ]}
/>

<Gif
  name="rouge"
  alt="A reference summary and a candidate summary of seven words each are laid out in boxes. Words that appear in both light up green. Five of the seven reference words are found in the candidate, so the toy ROUGE-1 recall is 5 over 7, or 0.71. A caption cites the 3,000 hours that manual evaluation would need."
  caption="Fig. 122: Toy summaries. ROUGE-1 counts overlapping words."
/>

<Convo
  lines={[
    { who: 'sundu', text: 'Who came up with it?' },
    { who: 'sriku', text: 'Chin-Yew Lin at the Information Sciences Institute, University of Southern California, in 2004. ROUGE automatically determines the quality of a summary by comparing it to ideal summaries created by humans, counting overlapping units such as n-grams, word sequences and word pairs. The paper introduces four measures: ROUGE-N, ROUGE-L, ROUGE-W and ROUGE-S.' },
    { who: 'cherry', text: 'Why not just ask people?' },
    { who: 'sriku', text: 'Lin estimates that even simple manual evaluation of summaries at the scale of the Document Understanding Conference would take over 3,000 hours of human effort, which is very expensive. Three of the measures were used in DUC 2004.' },
    { who: 'sundu', text: 'Is it the final word on quality?' },
    { who: 'sriku', text: 'No. Stiennon and colleagues note that summarization models are often trained to predict reference summaries and evaluated using ROUGE, but both are rough proxies for summary quality. They report that optimizing their human-feedback reward model produced better summaries than optimizing ROUGE, according to humans.' },
  ]}
/>

<CaseStudy
  title="ROUGE: A Package for Automatic Evaluation of Summaries (Lin, 2004)"
  stats={[
    { value: '4', label: 'Measures introduced: ROUGE-N, ROUGE-L, ROUGE-W and ROUGE-S' },
    { value: '3,000+ hours', label: 'Of human effort that manual evaluation at DUC scale would need' },
  ]}
  winner="The abstract: “It includes measures to automatically determine the quality of a summary by comparing it to other (ideal) summaries created by humans.”"
  takeaway="ROUGE is a cheap ruler, not a judge. Use it to rank quickly, and ask people before you decide."
  source={{
    text: 'Lin, “ROUGE: A Package for Automatic Evaluation of Summaries”, ACL Workshop 2004. Free PDF on the ACL Anthology.',
    href: 'https://aclanthology.org/W04-1013.pdf',
  }}
/>

**Real definition:** a family of metrics that evaluate automatic summarization and machine translation models. ROUGE metrics determine the degree to which a reference text overlaps an ML model’s generated text. [Google’s glossary: ROUGE ↗](https://developers.google.com/machine-learning/glossary#rouge-recall-oriented-understudy-for-gisting-evaluation)
